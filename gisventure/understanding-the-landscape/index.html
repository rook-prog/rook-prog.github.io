Why understanding the landscape matters in mangrove restoration

mangroves

essay
Planting asks where the seedlings can go. Restoration asks why they should survive there.
Author

Srijith Balan

Published

October 1, 2026

Whenever we hear the term mangrove restoration, the first image that usually comes to mind is quite simple: people standing on a mudflat, planting mangrove seedlings.

It is a positive image. It shows participation, effort and, more importantly, intent. But the more I work with coastal landscapes, the more I feel that this image represents only one part of restoration. In many cases, planting is probably not where the real work begins.

The real work begins much earlier, with trying to understand the landscape.

Why should a mangrove survive here?

This is probably one of the first questions we should ask before deciding where to plant. A mudflat may look uniform when we stand on it. From a distance, it may simply appear as one continuous open surface. But environmentally, it may not be uniform at all.

A slight difference in elevation, the way tidal water enters and leaves, how long water remains in an area, whether the sediment is stable or loose, salinity conditions, exposure, drainage and existing vegetation can all change within relatively short distances. To us, it may be the same mudflat. To a mangrove seedling, it may be a completely different environment.

That is where the distinction between planting mangroves and restoring mangroves becomes important.

Planting asks: Where can we put the seedlings?

Restoration asks: Why should they survive there, and what needs to be functioning in that landscape for them to survive naturally?

Sometimes the problem may not be the absence of trees

We often see a bare coastal area and immediately think that vegetation needs to be introduced. But an open intertidal area is not necessarily an empty or degraded landscape.

Mudflats, tidal creeks, shallow water areas, saltmarshes and mangrove patches can all be parts of the same coastal system. Trying to convert every available open space into a plantation can therefore become problematic. Some areas may genuinely require intervention. Some may recover naturally if the environmental conditions are protected. Some may require restoration of water connectivity or other site conditions before planting is even considered. And some areas may simply need to be left alone.

I think this is one of the more important shifts required in restoration thinking. The question should not always be “Where can we plant?” Sometimes the better question is “What is this landscape trying to do naturally?”

GIS and remote sensing can tell us much more than where to plant

When GIS or remote sensing is mentioned in restoration projects, it is often associated with producing maps: suitable area, unsuitable area, plantation zone, priority zone.

Those outputs are useful, but I think their real value lies deeper. They help us understand how the landscape has behaved, not just how it looks at one point in time.

Satellite images allow us to look back and examine how the shoreline has changed, where vegetation persisted or disappeared, where channels shifted, where sediment accumulated, and whether an area that appears stable today has actually changed repeatedly over the years. Terrain data can help us understand elevation differences that may not be obvious in the field, while field observations tell us where our interpretation from satellite data was correct and where it was not.

Soil and sediment observations add another layer. Hydrology adds another. Local knowledge adds yet another. Individually, none of these tells the full story. Together, they begin to describe how the landscape actually functions.

For me, this is where geospatial analysis becomes particularly useful, not because it can always give us a final answer, but because it can help us ask better questions.

A map should also show us what we do not know

This is something I have started appreciating more through project work. There is always a temptation to make a final map look definitive.

Green means suitable. Red means unsuitable. Yellow means moderate.

Simple, clean and easy to communicate.

But natural systems are rarely that clean. There are places where the available information is not enough, locations where field conditions do not match what we initially interpreted, and areas where seasonal behaviour may completely change how we understand the site.

Sometimes the correct conclusion may simply be: we need more information before intervening.

That uncertainty should not be treated as a weakness. In many cases, recognising uncertainty is probably more responsible than forcing an answer from incomplete data. A useful restoration map should therefore not only tell us where intervention appears possible. It should also tell us where verification is needed, where uncertainty is high, and where intervention may carry unnecessary risk.

And then there are the people who live with the landscape

Technical assessments can become very data-heavy very quickly: satellite imagery, elevation models, hydrology, sediment, salinity, vegetation indices, field measurements. All of these matter.

But coastal landscapes are not just physical systems. They are also lived landscapes.

People fish there, travel through them, collect resources and observe seasonal changes. They notice when a tidal creek becomes shallower, when sediment begins accumulating in a particular area, when vegetation disappears, or when an earlier plantation survives in one location but fails completely in another.

That knowledge may never appear clearly in a satellite image.

This does not mean local observations should replace scientific assessment, nor should scientific assessment dismiss local observations. The more useful approach is probably to allow both to inform each other. Local observations can tell us where to look more carefully, geospatial data can place those observations in a wider spatial or temporal context, and field verification can test whether our interpretation actually holds.

Restoration, then, becomes a process rather than an event

Instead of thinking:

Plant → Protect

I increasingly see restoration as something closer to:

Observe → Understand → Decide → Intervene → Monitor → Learn

That final part, learn, matters because our original assumptions may turn out to be wrong. A site that looked suitable may perform poorly. A place we expected to struggle may recover naturally. A planted patch may survive, but natural regeneration around it may remain absent. A hydrological intervention may alter the landscape differently from what was anticipated.

Monitoring should not simply confirm whether a project succeeded. It should also tell us whether our understanding of the landscape was correct. And if it was not, then the restoration strategy should be allowed to change.

Planting numbers are easy. Restoration success is harder.

Numbers communicate well. Ten thousand seedlings planted. Fifty hectares covered. One lakh propagules distributed. They sound substantial, and they are easy to report.

But they do not necessarily tell us whether an ecosystem is recovering.

A smaller intervention carried out in an environmentally appropriate location may sometimes achieve much more than a larger plantation implemented without understanding site conditions. So perhaps the more meaningful questions are whether the plants are surviving and growing, whether natural recruitment is beginning, whether tidal processes are functioning properly, whether the substrate remains stable, whether the area is becoming ecologically self-sustaining, and whether the local community remains connected to the restoration process.

Those questions take longer to answer, but they probably tell us much more.

Before we plant

I am not arguing against mangrove plantation. Planting can be an important restoration intervention.

But it should come after understanding the landscape, not before it.

The objective should not be to make every exposed coastal surface green. It should be to understand what that landscape can support, what it should retain, what has been disturbed, and where intervention is actually required.

Sometimes restoration may mean planting. Sometimes it may mean protecting natural regeneration. Sometimes it may mean correcting an environmental constraint. And sometimes it may mean leaving an area undisturbed.

Perhaps, then, the success of mangrove restoration should not ultimately be measured only by how many propagules we put into the ground. It should also be measured by how well we understood the landscape before we put them there, and how carefully we continued listening to it afterwards.

If you are involved in mangrove plantation or restoration work, I would be interested to know how you approach this.

What do you think of looking at restoration this way, with site understanding coming before intervention?

And beyond the aspects I have mentioned here, what else do you think should be considered before deciding whether to plant, restore hydrology, support natural regeneration, or simply leave an area undisturbed?

I would be especially interested in perspectives from people working directly in the field, because these decisions often become much more complicated once we move from maps and plans to the actual landscape.
